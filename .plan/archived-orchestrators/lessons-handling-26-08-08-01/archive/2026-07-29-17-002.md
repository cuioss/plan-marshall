# A plan-ID renumber in one epic silently orphans citations in another

## What happened

An orchestrator epic's `epic.md` carries a **generated** START-HERE block — explicitly marked
"never hand-write or hand-edit", regenerated from `status.json` via `orchestrator resume-summary`
and pasted back between markers.

Nothing forces that regeneration. The block drifted badly: it advertised `R=6 of N=6` when the
machine authority said 5, listed a plan as launched that had shipped, and — the damaging part —
listed a **queue of plan IDs that no longer existed** in `status.json` (`PLAN-61`, `PLAN-64`,
`PLAN-104`, and others, dropped or re-slugged out of `plans[]`).

Prose elsewhere in the ledger then cited those dead IDs as if they were live work:

- an Open Defect entry claiming "PLAN-64/104 own the reconciliation" — so a real, three-times-observed
  defect was recorded as **owned** while actually being unowned,
- two plan specs naming a dead ID as the adjacent plan to check surface-disjointness against, one of
  them in a plan that was **already launched**, so the correction could no longer reach its ingested
  `request.md`.

None of it surfaced on its own. It surfaced only because an unrelated running plan tripped over the
underlying defect and the operator pasted the observation back.

⚠ **The first diagnosis was itself wrong.** The prose attributed the dead IDs to a *sibling epic's*
renumbering, and that story was recorded as fact before being checked. Reading the stale generated
block showed the IDs had been **this epic's own** rows. A cross-epic citation that *was* still valid
(`PLAN-03`) proved the citations were not uniformly stale either — each had to be resolved
individually against the live queue.

## Why it matters

A citation naming a nonexistent plan is **indistinguishable at read time from one naming a real
plan**. Both read as "this is owned, someone else has it." A phantom owner is strictly worse than no
owner: an unowned defect at least looks unowned, whereas a phantom owner actively suppresses
re-triage. This defect accumulated three observations while reading as assigned.

The deeper trap is that the drifted artifact was a **generated** one. Generated artifacts carry
borrowed authority — they look derived, therefore current, therefore quotable. But "generated" only
means *it was rendered at some point*; without a trigger it is a **snapshot**, and a snapshot of a
mutable queue is a fossil that reads like a fact.

## How to apply

- **Never cite a rendered/generated view as a source.** Cite the machine authority (`status.json`).
  A generated block is a convenience for humans, not an addressable record.
- If a generated block is marked "regenerate after every X", treat the absence of an enforcing
  mechanism as a defect in its own right. "Regenerate after every queue-touching change" is a hope
  unless a script or a test enforces it.
- **Never cite another epic's work by bare ID** — cite the `slug`, which survives renumbering, and
  re-resolve the ID against the live queue before acting on it.
- **Re-resolve every citation before it is used to justify NOT doing work.** "Owned by PLAN-NN" is a
  claim to verify, not a fact.
- When repairing a citation by inference (slug similarity), **label the successor inferred and
  unconfirmed** rather than silently substituting a new number — a confidently-wrong ID repeats the
  original failure.
- ⛔ **Do not record a causal story you have not verified.** "The sibling renumbered" was plausible,
  explained every symptom, and was wrong. Symptom-fit is not evidence. State what was observed
  (the IDs are absent from both queues) separately from why (unverified).
- Correcting a spec for an **already-launched** plan does not reach it — the request body was
  ingested at init. Record that the correction did not propagate rather than assuming it did.
